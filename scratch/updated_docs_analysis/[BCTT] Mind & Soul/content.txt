Oat vs. dairy milk: Nutrition, benefits, and downsides

If you're allergic to cow's milk or follow a vegan diet, oat milk can be a good alternative.

October 22, 2026

By Julie Corliss, Executive Editor, Harvard Heart Letter

Reviewed by Lauren Abeles, MS, RD, LDN, Contributor

With its mild flavor and creamy texture, oat milk has become one of the most popular plant-based milks in recent years. But how does it stack up against regular cow’s milk? Here’s what to consider before buying a carton of oat milk or requesting it for your routine coffee order.

Is oat milk healthier than cow’s milk?

“If you have a true milk allergy — an abnormal, potentially serious immune system reaction to the proteins found in cow’s milk — oat milk is a healthier alternative to regular milk,” says Lauren Abeles, a registered dietitian at Harvard-affiliated Brigham and Women’s Faulkner Hospital. Oat milk is also free from other common allergens, such as soy and nuts, which are other common foods used to make plant-based milks.

For people who are vegan (that is, they strictly avoid all animal-based products), oat milk is one good option. But it’s not necessarily healthier than other plant-based milks, says Abeles. Soy milk, for example, contains 7 grams of protein per cup — nearly as much protein as cow’s milk and more than twice as much as oat milk.

Some people have trouble digesting a natural sugar in cow’s milk called lactose. People with this problem (known as lactose intolerance) can experience diarrhea, bloating, or gas after consuming dairy products. Oat and other plant-based milks don’t contain lactose. However, people who are sensitive to lactose can also opt for lactose-free milk, which may be preferable for either taste or nutrition reasons, says Abeles.

How do oat milk and cow’s milk compare nutritionally?

In addition to having less protein than dairy milk, oat milk contains more carbohydrates (see “Nutrients in cow’s milk vs. oat milk”). While the total fat levels are similar, cow’s milk has more saturated fat. Oat milk contains a small amount of fiber, while cow’s milk has none. Most oat milks are fortified with calcium and vitamin D at levels similar to those found in cow’s milk. But there are no set standards and the amounts can vary between brands, so check the label.

Your personal health and dietary needs can affect which milk makes the most sense for you. “If you’re trying to lose weight, you’re better off eating more protein and fewer carbs, so cow’s milk may be a better choice,” says Abeles. That’s also true for people with diabetes or prediabetes, since the carbs in oat milk can contribute to blood sugar elevations, she adds. Compared to cow’s milk, the per serving difference in carbs from oat milk may not seem that large. But the total amount adds up if you put oat milk in your cereal and coffee and use it for cooking, Abeles points out.

Of course, if you have several servings of 2% cow’s milk daily, the saturated fat adds up, too. According to the American Heart Association, people who need to lower their LDL cholesterol should limit their total daily saturated intake to 6% of their total daily calories, which translates to about 13 grams or less.

A new start after 60

A new start after 60: At 26, I saw my dream Porsche. At 68, I built one from scratch

Bill Drysdale could never afford the sports car he fell in love with as a young man. So when he retired, he went to night school to learn welding, and began assembling it, bit by bit ...

vowed, “I will have one of those.” As the years went by, the price of Drysdale’s dream car rose above £1m. “I realised I was never going to be able to buy one.” But when he retired, still harbouring the dream, he thought: “Why don’t I make one?”

Drysdale grew up in Wigtown, Scotland, and cars were in his blood – his grandfather was chauffeur to the local creamery manager, and he first saw a race with his father, when he was 12 – around the time that his mother went into hospital with multiple sclerosis. Drysdale, an only child, grew close to his father. At 14, he taught him to drive. Together they’d huddle in the kitchen to listen to the Le Mans 24 Hour race on the radio.

Drysdale was only 21 when his dad died; his mother ended up staying in hospital for 23 years. “That made me very independent,” he says. Determined? “Pig-headed, some would say.”

After studying maths and physics at the University of Glasgow, he joined a brake manufacturer as a graduate apprentice engineer. “Oh, it was tremendous,” he says. “As young chaps in the company, there was always this interest in cars and racing and competition.” His favoured form was “sprinting – racing against the clock, competing against yourself”. It was while playing mechanic to a friend in the pit at Silverstone that he saw the Porsche 904 and was floored by “the sound, the look, the line”.

Drysdale married and became father to a son and a daughter, at which point he stopped motor racing. While his wife stayed at home in Warwickshire, England, he travelled widely for work – India, South Africa, Brazil, Japan, Argentina, Europe, Australia. By the time he retired at the age of 58, he was international president for a Canadian company, and moved back to Scotland. It was here that he had the idea to build his own version of the famous Porsche.

Drysdale went to night school for six months to learn how to weld. Through a friend of a friend of a friend, he got a blueprint of the original Porsche 904. He made the shape of the body in his garage, first from wood, then plaster of paris. From this he made fibreglass moulds; his wife, Lucia, helped him to make the resin. Next came the doors, a bonnet, front and rear wings, the interior and instrument panel.

“Once I had the body, I could concentrate on the engineering, fabricating the chassis, designing the suspension. That was all from scratch,” he says. He bought a secondhand engine and transmission – “then started to put all the parts together”.

Drysdale driving his ‘Porsche 904FF’. Photograph: Joy Richings

The children “thought I was off my rocker”, he says. Sometimes, the project would defeat him. “For a couple of months I would not go into the garage at all. Then the enthusiasm would come back … I’ve always been interested in broadening myself.” After eight years, the car was finished; Drysdale was 68. He calls his car a Porsche 904FF. The double Fs stand for “effing fake”.

When it was done, he transported the car to test days at race tracks, where he did laps to set up the handling. In Scotland, England, France and Switzerland, he competed in speed hill climbs and sprints, right until February this year, when at 87 a stroke affected his sight and put paid to driving.

“I’m coming to terms with it but it is very difficult,” he says. Meanwhile, the car continues to compete, driven by friends, while Drysdale takes part as “entrant”. He is already planning the next season. “I intend to be in the paddock, in the pit, next year with my race car,with someone else driving it.”

“I think my dad would have said: ‘Yep, that’s my son,’” he says. “The car has given me a dream. I’m a great believer that you must have a dream. A dream to me is something you really would like to do but don’t know if you ever will. That’s my philosophy in life. Look forward. And do the things that you want to do.”

The women’s boxing nights helping abuse survivors

Words by Sarah Keenlyside

November 2, 2026

A new series of all-female fight nights aims to fund free boxing classes that help women rebuild their confidence after abusive relationships

“The Fight Forward programme literally saved my life,” explains Saria, 40, raising her voice above the sound of gloves hitting punchbags and fellow boxers chatting at the 12×3 boxing gym in Aldgate, east London. 

Two years ago, after leaving one abusive relationship and then finding herself in another, Saria was struggling. She signed up for her first boxing camp, run by former Team GB boxer and European gold medallist Lesley Sackey, who had begun offering free classes to survivors of domestic abuse.

“Doing that camp gave me the strength to fight for myself in that relationship, to get myself out of it, and to have confidence,” she recalls. “It gave me a sense of self-worth that I’d never had in my life.”

As she speaks, others from her cohort sidle up beside her, flushed and energised after rounds of sparring, to compare notes on everything from gum shields to home-made glove deodorising sprays.

Saria’s involvement in boxing now goes well beyond a weekly fitness class: on 28 November she will join several women from the programme to step into the ring at London’s Ladbroke Hall for The Fight Back. It will be the inaugural event staged by a new venture called Fight Back Promotions.

Founded by Sackey alongside three-time national boxing champion Shelly Sweeney and impact media entrepreneur Poppy Mason-Watts, Fight Back Promotions aims to turn ticketed events into a sustainable source of funding for Fight Forward CIC, the not-for-profit organisation behind Saria’s training programme.

A quarter of the profits from each event will support Fight Forward’s classes and programmes for women affected by domestic abuse. The founders also want to give women in white-collar boxing more opportunities to compete against evenly matched opponents, in a space where men have traditionally dominated fight cards.

Women were barred from professional boxing in the UK until 1998, while women’s boxing made its Olympic debut at London 2012. In white-collar boxing, the organisers say, women can still find themselves matched with opponents in different weight classes or with different levels of experience.

Serving women better “means taking into account fair matching,” Sackey explains. “It also means allowances around period weight. That’s a massive thing. Boxing is a weight-class sport, and we know that when it’s our cycle, we can put on up to 2kg in water weight. That’s never been considered in the white-collar space before.”

For Mason-Watts, securing big-name sponsors was an important part of establishing the project. Brands including lululemon, NEOM and Proper Snacks have signed up to support the events, but ticket sales remain central to the model. She hopes the first fight night will fund free places for another 40 to 60 women, with the team aiming to stage two events a year.

The WHO estimates that nearly one in three women worldwide have experienced physical or sexual violence by an intimate partner, or sexual violence by someone else, during their lifetime. Meanwhile, the latest figures from the ONS suggest that around one in eight women aged 16 and over in England and Wales experienced domestic abuse, sexual victimisation or stalking in the past year.

Fight Back co-founders, from left to right, Poppy Mason-Watts, Lesley Sackey and Shelly Sweeney. Image: Fight Back

Just fewer than 40 women have now completed the structured boxing programme, with organisers gathering feedback before and after sessions to assess changes in participants’ mental health and self-esteem. Sackey says the team is also working with refuges and domestic abuse charities to establish a referral pathway, while some women continue training long after their initial camp has ended.

Sweeney, who coaches many of the classes, says she sees the change from the side of the ring. “A few women have come in quite shy, with curved shoulders, and over time, as they box more, their chest lifts slightly, their chin comes up, and you suddenly notice they’re the ones starting conversations rather than having conversations being started for them.”

For women who have experienced domestic abuse, boxing might seem an unlikely way to rebuild confidence, particularly when the sport is so closely associated with physical violence. Fight Forward’s free introductory classes, however, do not require participants to spar or compete, and women can train at a pace that suits them.

There’s a misconception that they’re putting women back into the space of violence that they’ve experienced before. But this is different. This is a consensual environment

Fight Forward participant Amja has heard that objection before. “There’s a misconception that they’re putting women back into the space of violence that they’ve experienced before,” she says. “But this is different. This is a consensual environment. It’s controlled, you have choice, and it’s a way of releasing tension. In a violent situation, you have no choice, and your autonomy is taken away from you.”

“My children were at my fight night,” she adds, “and I think them witnessing their mother in a different light was a game-changer, considering the dynamic of the separation.”

For Saria, 28 November will be her fourth fight in the ring. She has been told who her opponent will be, although they have never met, which she says is beside the point. “It’s not about getting into the ring and fighting somebody else. It’s about fighting for me, because I am worth fighting for.

Founded in 1961, Tay Ho banh cuon restaurant in Ho Chi Minh City has earned Michelin's Bib Gourmand recognition for its traditional Vietnamese steamed rice rolls. Its owner also welcomes diners in a different ao dai every day.

A family recipe passed down through generations

On a weekend morning, 66-year-old Lan traveled from her home in the former District 7 to Tay Ho banh cuon restaurant in Tan Dinh Ward, Ho Chi Minh City, to enjoy a familiar dish.

Lan has been a regular customer since the 1980s.

For her, the restaurant holds memories of her younger years. Although she no longer lives nearby, she still returns whenever she has the opportunity.

Lan enjoys banh cuon at her longtime favorite restaurant on a weekend morning.

Seated not far from Lan were Tuyen, born in 1984, and her Singaporean friend Carmen, born in 1985.

Tuyen has also known the restaurant for many years.

"This banh cuon restaurant has been famous for a long time. The rice rolls are delicious, and the dipping sauce is just right," she said. "Carmen also loves Vietnamese banh cuon and banh gio (steamed rice dumplings). Whenever I have the chance, I bring her here for banh cuon."

Restaurant owner Tran Thi Thuy Loan, born in 1974, said the business was established in 1961 by her late mother-in-law, Tran Thi Ca.

Ca initially operated a small roadside stall beside a temple dedicated to Vietnamese patriot and reformer Phan Chau Trinh on Gallimard Street, now Nguyen Huy Tu Street in Da Kao Ward, formerly part of District 1.

"Phan Chau Trinh was also known by the pen name Tay Ho, so people often called him Phan Tay Ho," Loan explained. "Customers began referring to my mother-in-law's stall as Tay Ho banh cuon to make it easier to remember and find. She liked the name and decided to keep it."

Restaurant owner Loan says Tay Ho was established in 1961.

When the restaurant later moved to a new location, it retained the original name.

As Ca grew older, Loan and other family members gradually took over the business. For the past three years, Loan has been primarily responsible for running it.

Throughout the decades, she has preserved the traditional banh cuon recipe handed down by her mother-in-law.

The dish consists of delicate steamed rice-flour sheets wrapped around a filling of pork, jicama and wood ear mushrooms.

To accommodate different tastes, Loan has expanded the menu beyond the original recipe.

Customers can now choose rice rolls filled with shrimp, pork and mushrooms; chicken and mushrooms; shredded pork floss; or shrimp floss.

The restaurant also offers vegetarian varieties, including mixed rice rolls filled with mushrooms, lotus seeds and beans; pandan-flavored rice rolls with vegetables and tofu; and rolls filled with lingzhi mushrooms.

In June 2026, Tay Ho received a Bib Gourmand distinction from the Michelin Guide, which recognizes restaurants offering good food at reasonable prices.

Michelin praised the restaurant's thin, tender and light rice rolls, noting that the ingredients are prepared beside an open kitchen and that the traditional pork-and-jicama filling is complemented by vegetarian options.

The restaurant operates daily from 5:30 a.m. to 9:30 p.m.

Depending on the filling, dishes cost VND40,000-72,000 (approximately $1.50-2.75) per serving.

An owner who welcomes customers in traditional ao dai

With its flavorful rice rolls and diverse menu, Tay Ho attracts a steady stream of customers.

What began as an unnamed roadside stall has become a well-known two-story restaurant popular with both Vietnamese and international diners.

Many longtime customers first visited when Ca was still preparing and selling the rice rolls.

Today, some return with their own children to enjoy a dish closely connected to their childhood memories.

Tay Ho received Michelin's Bib Gourmand recognition in June 2026.

"I remember two elderly customers in particular," Loan said. "Although they lived very far away, they still traveled here to eat. They told me they came because they missed the old flavors and wanted to revisit memories of their youth."

Younger customers also visit, telling Loan that their parents used to bring them to the restaurant when they were children.

Some have since moved abroad but make a point of returning whenever they visit Vietnam.

"To prove it, they even show me photographs of themselves eating here when they were little," she said.

The restaurant has also attracted growing numbers of international visitors, particularly since receiving Michelin recognition.

Depending on the filling, banh cuon dishes cost VND40,000-72,000 per serving.

To better serve foreign customers, Loan previously spent her evenings studying foreign languages with a teacher after finishing work.

"Thanks to those lessons, I can now serve and talk with international customers," she said. "Many of them stay in touch with me after returning to their home countries. They also recommend the restaurant to friends and relatives. That makes me very touched and happy."

Beyond its food, Tay Ho has become known for Loan's distinctive approach to welcoming guests.

Every day, she arrives at the restaurant wearing a traditional Vietnamese ao dai, greeting customers with a smile as she prepares freshly steamed rice rolls.

The colors and patterns of her outfits change from day to day. She rarely wears the same color twice during a week.

"I believe dressing beautifully and respectfully shouldn't be reserved for parties or special outings," Loan explained. "Even when selling food, we should dress neatly and elegantly to show respect for our customers."

For her, the choice of clothing also carries cultural significance.

"Banh cuon is a traditional Vietnamese dish, and the ao dai is our traditional costume," she said. "I wear an ao dai while serving banh cuon both to show respect for diners and to promote the beauty of Vietnamese culture."

Loan admitted that she initially felt self-conscious wearing the traditional outfit while working in the kitchen.

"But everyone who came to the restaurant complimented me. They said it looked beautiful, elegant and perfectly suited to the food we serve," she recalled. "So, for the past three years, I've worn a different ao dai every day I come to work."
