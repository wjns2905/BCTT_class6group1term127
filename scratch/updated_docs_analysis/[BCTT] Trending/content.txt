Improving livelihoods through forest protection

Deputy Ta Thi Yen from Dien Bien province highlighted the strategic importance of the northern midland and mountainous region in national defence, security safeguarding and upstream forest protection, while noting the hardships faced by local residents, particularly ethnic minority communities.

She said development policies should recognise their contributions and provide better livelihoods and incomes for people involved in forest protection.

She called for integrated transport infrastructure linking production areas with processing facilities, border gates and markets, alongside stronger incentives for businesses investing in agricultural processing, preservation and logistics in mountainous areas.

For the northern midland and mountainous region, it is necessary to continue capitalising on local advantages of agriculture, specialties, forestry, medicinal plants, processing industry, and tourism associated with natural landscapes and the cultural identity of ethnic minorities, the deputy added.

Preparing clean land for border-gate economic zones

Deputy Doan Thi Le An from Cao Bang province called for improved policies to develop the border-gate economy in mountainous and border areas, focusing on clean land preparation and investor selection.

She said international border gates such as Ta Lung and Tra Linh, together with the Dong Dang–Tra Linh expressway project, offer Cao Bang opportunities to expand trade with China and ASEAN. She added that the mechanism should clearly define its scope, prioritise essential infrastructure, ensure adequate compensation and resettlement arrangements, and provide transparent oversight of land use and implementation.

She stressed that zoning plans must not replace legal requirements for land acquisition and that land preparation must protect residents’ livelihoods, particularly those of ethnic minority communities in border areas.

An called for clear selection criteria covering financial capacity, experience, disbursement commitments, job creation and contributions to local development, alongside transparent supervision and accountability.

It was a hot night in 1989 when my wallet – stuffed with $1,000 cash – fell out of my pocket.

After university, I left the UK and went travelling in Australia for a year. I saw the delights of the east coast – mostly from various building sites, as I worked my way around doing long hours in the blistering sun. But I loved every minute of it. After 10 months, I had saved enough to make my way slowly back home.

I was enjoying one last night out in Darwin with a gang of traveller friends with whom I was flying to Timor the next day. The intention was to island hop all the way to Singapore.

Cash was king in those days, and the $1,000 I had in my wallet was enough to last me a month on the road. The beers went down easy and I was completely unaware when, at some point in the evening, I lost my wallet. I was still none the wiser when I went to bed to get some sleep before the flight the next morning.

At 1.30am, I awoke to a loud knocking on my door. I stumbled out of bed, bleary-eyed, and opened the door to the hostel manager and an elderly couple – who were holding my wallet.

That couple had spent hours trawling every backpacker place in Darwin (they’d been to four!) before eventually finding me, care of my student ID card. Every dollar was still there.

Still half asleep, and worse for wear after the evening’s drinks, I didn’t fully appreciate the extent of their kindness in the moment. I thanked them and went back to bed, but it wasn’t until the next morning that I realised the incredible lengths they had gone to in order to return my wallet. Without them, my trip would have been ruined.

I’ve always wished I could thank them properly, but in lieu of that I made a pledge to myself – and in a way, to them – that I would always go that extra mile to help people when I have the chance.


When I do get the opportunity to lend someone a hand, I always tell them to pay it forward. So I like to think that kind couple in Darwin have actually helped countless others, too.

A new start after 60

A new start after 60: At 26, I saw my dream Porsche. At 68, I built one from scratch

Bill Drysdale could never afford the sports car he fell in love with as a young man. So when he retired, he went to night school to learn welding, and began assembling it, bit by bit ...

vowed, “I will have one of those.” As the years went by, the price of Drysdale’s dream car rose above £1m. “I realised I was never going to be able to buy one.” But when he retired, still harbouring the dream, he thought: “Why don’t I make one?”

Drysdale grew up in Wigtown, Scotland, and cars were in his blood – his grandfather was chauffeur to the local creamery manager, and he first saw a race with his father, when he was 12 – around the time that his mother went into hospital with multiple sclerosis. Drysdale, an only child, grew close to his father. At 14, he taught him to drive. Together they’d huddle in the kitchen to listen to the Le Mans 24 Hour race on the radio.

Drysdale was only 21 when his dad died; his mother ended up staying in hospital for 23 years. “That made me very independent,” he says. Determined? “Pig-headed, some would say.”

After studying maths and physics at the University of Glasgow, he joined a brake manufacturer as a graduate apprentice engineer. “Oh, it was tremendous,” he says. “As young chaps in the company, there was always this interest in cars and racing and competition.” His favoured form was “sprinting – racing against the clock, competing against yourself”. It was while playing mechanic to a friend in the pit at Silverstone that he saw the Porsche 904 and was floored by “the sound, the look, the line”.

Drysdale married and became father to a son and a daughter, at which point he stopped motor racing. While his wife stayed at home in Warwickshire, England, he travelled widely for work – India, South Africa, Brazil, Japan, Argentina, Europe, Australia. By the time he retired at the age of 58, he was international president for a Canadian company, and moved back to Scotland. It was here that he had the idea to build his own version of the famous Porsche.

Drysdale went to night school for six months to learn how to weld. Through a friend of a friend of a friend, he got a blueprint of the original Porsche 904. He made the shape of the body in his garage, first from wood, then plaster of paris. From this he made fibreglass moulds; his wife, Lucia, helped him to make the resin. Next came the doors, a bonnet, front and rear wings, the interior and instrument panel.

“Once I had the body, I could concentrate on the engineering, fabricating the chassis, designing the suspension. That was all from scratch,” he says. He bought a secondhand engine and transmission – “then started to put all the parts together”.

Drysdale driving his ‘Porsche 904FF’. Photograph: Joy Richings

The children “thought I was off my rocker”, he says. Sometimes, the project would defeat him. “For a couple of months I would not go into the garage at all. Then the enthusiasm would come back … I’ve always been interested in broadening myself.” After eight years, the car was finished; Drysdale was 68. He calls his car a Porsche 904FF. The double Fs stand for “effing fake”.

When it was done, he transported the car to test days at race tracks, where he did laps to set up the handling. In Scotland, England, France and Switzerland, he competed in speed hill climbs and sprints, right until February this year, when at 87 a stroke affected his sight and put paid to driving.

“I’m coming to terms with it but it is very difficult,” he says. Meanwhile, the car continues to compete, driven by friends, while Drysdale takes part as “entrant”. He is already planning the next season. “I intend to be in the paddock, in the pit, next year with my race car, with someone else driving it.”

“I think my dad would have said: ‘Yep, that’s my son,’” he says. “The car has given me a dream. I’m a great believer that you must have a dream. A dream to me is something you really would like to do but don’t know if you ever will. That’s my philosophy in life. Look forward. And do the things that you want to do.”

500-day-and-night campaign: DNA testing results returned for nearly 800 samples of martyrs’ remains 

After seven months of the 500-day-and-night campaign, relevant units have completed DNA testing and returned results for nearly 800 samples of martyrs’ remains, successfully identifying dozens of fallen soldiers. Each result reflects the dedication and sense of responsibility of the participating forces, while bringing great comfort to the families of those who sacrificed their lives for the Fatherland.

Saturday, October 17, 2026 at 22:30

Ceremony marking the transfer of samples of martyrs’ remains collected from provinces and cities from an aircraft at Gia Lam Airport in Ha Noi on September 11, 2026, for handover and DNA testing. (Photo: THE DAI)

Bringing great comfort to families of fallen soldiers

Sharing information on the current situation and results of efforts to identify martyrs’ remains through DNA testing, Ngo Kieu Lien, Head of the Data and Information Division for People with Meritorious Services under the Department of People with Meritorious Services, Ministry of Home Affairs, said that searching for, collecting and identifying martyrs’ remains is an important and sacred political task that has consistently received attention and direction from the Party and State.

Under current regulations, the identification of martyrs’ remains is carried out using two methods: identification based on factual evidence and DNA testing. DNA testing has been implemented since 2013 under Project 150, approved by the Prime Minister through Decision No. 150/QD-TTg dated January 14, 2013, on identifying martyrs’ remains with missing information. In practice, this has proved to be a highly reliable scientific method, making an important contribution to restoring the identities of fallen soldiers.

As the 500-day-and-night campaign to accelerate the search for, collection and identification of martyrs’ remains is being implemented, ministries, sectors, localities and DNA testing units have worked closely together and made considerable efforts.

Specifically, during the first seven months of the campaign, from March 15 to October 15, 2026, relevant units completed DNA testing and returned results for nearly 800 samples of martyrs’ remains, successfully identifying dozens of fallen soldiers. The results have been respectfully delivered to their relatives.

Each result reflects the dedication and sense of responsibility of the participating forces, while bringing great comfort to the families of fallen soldiers.

An overview of Nhan Dan Newspaper’s online discussion, “Looking back at the achievements of more than 200 days and nights of efforts to bring fallen soldiers’ home”. (Photo: MINH DUY)

Lien shared this information during an online discussion entitled “Looking back at the achievements of more than 200 days and nights of efforts to bring fallen soldiers home”, organised by Nhan Dan Newspaper.

However, according to the head of the Data and Information Division for People with Meritorious Services, these results remain modest compared with the number of unidentified martyrs’ remains and the expectations of their relatives.This is mainly due to various objective difficulties, which relevant authorities are working together to address.

Under the assignment of the National Steering Committee for the Search, Collection and Identification of Martyrs’ Remains (National Steering Committee 515), the Ministry of Home Affairs will organise inspection teams at designated DNA testing units during the final months of 2026.

These include the Military Institute of Forensic Medicine under the Ministry of National Defence, the National Institute of Forensic Medicine under the Ministry of Health, the Institute of Biology under the Viet Nam Academy of Science and Technology, the Institute of Criminal Science under the Ministry of Public Security, and the Hanoi Steering Committee 515.

The inspections will focus on progress in receiving, analysing and returning results for samples of martyrs’ remains, as well as difficulties, obstacles and solutions to promptly resolve problems or recommend action by competent authorities, ensuring the effective implementation of tasks in the coming period.

At the Ha Noi Steering Committee 515, the inspection team will specifically examine the results of collecting samples of martyrs’ remains at martyrs’ cemeteries, as well as sample preservation and handover procedures.

Towards improving DNA testing capacity for martyrs’ remains

Ngo Kieu Lien said that, according to reports from the relevant units, DNA testing of martyrs’ remains still relies primarily on mitochondrial DNA analysis.

The Institute of Biology under the Viet Nam Academy of Science and Technology is piloting next-generation sequencing technology. Testing units have also applied methods for processing severely degraded bone and tooth samples. The expertise of specialists has gradually improved, while efforts to establish a genetic database for comparison are being accelerated.

Ngo Kieu Lien, Head of the Data and Information Division for People with Meritorious Services under the Department of People with Meritorious Services, Ministry of Home Affairs. (Photo: MINH DUY)

In particular, under the 500-day-and-night campaign, testing units are expected to receive new technology-based DNA testing systems from mid-October 2026, helping increase testing capacity.

Regarding difficulties and obstacles, practical experience and reports from relevant units show that DNA identification of martyrs’ remains is an exceptionally challenging field, even for countries with advanced scientific capabilities. Results depend on numerous factors, some of which are beyond human control.

First, sample quality remains a major challenge. Most remains have been buried for more than 50 years in acidic, humid soil under tropical climatic conditions, causing severe DNA degradation and fragmentation. Many samples contain only tiny amounts of bone or are contaminated. Some have undergone repeated analysis without yielding results.

Second, information about fallen soldiers on many graves has been lost or is inaccurate, making it extremely complicated to narrow down potential identities and conduct comparisons.

Third, the number of samples requiring analysis is enormous. Under the 500-day-and-night campaign, sample collection has been completed for nearly 150,000 samples of martyrs’ remains. Together with approximately 17,000 samples collected previously, the total has reached around 167,000, while the current capacity of testing units remains limited.

“We therefore hope that the families of fallen soldiers will understand and share these difficulties. Some cases require more time, while in others, identification may not yet be possible with current scientific capabilities. Nevertheless, we affirm that the relevant authorities will not give up. Samples and data will be preserved over the long term for further comparison when additional information becomes available or advances in technology make identification possible,” Ngo Kieu Lien said.

Key tasks of the 500-day-and-night campaign

1. Search for and collect approximately 7,000 sets of martyrs’ remains.

2. Complete the collection of samples from unidentified martyrs’ graves in martyrs’ cemeteries and from newly discovered and recovered remains.

3. Conduct DNA testing on approximately 18,000 samples of martyrs’ remains.

4. Develop, complete and put into operation a genetic database of relatives of unidentified fallen soldiers to facilitate DNA matching and identification of martyrs’ remains.

5. Accelerate and complete the clearance of landmines and unexploded ordnance to support the search for and collection of martyrs’ remains, focusing on priority areas and key locations such as Tuyen Quang Province, Lao Cai Province, Lang Son Province and other areas where information about martyrs’ graves is available.

The U.K. will increase the minimum maintenance funds international students must demonstrate for visa applications starting Nov. 30, with the maximum requirement rising by £369 (US$493).

Under updated Home Office immigration rules, the monthly maintenance requirement will rise from £1,529 to £1,570 for students studying in London. For those attending universities outside the capital, the requirement will increase from £1,171 to £1,203.

Students must prove they have sufficient funds for each month of their course, capped at nine months. This means applicants taking courses lasting nine months or longer will have to show £14,130 for living expenses in London, up from £13,761. Those studying outside London will need to show £10,827, compared with £10,539 previously.

The new financial thresholds apply based on the date an applicant submits their visa application, rather than the course start date. Applications submitted before Nov. 30 will be assessed under the existing rules.

This financial requirement is strictly for living costs and remains separate from tuition fees. Applicants must additionally demonstrate sufficient funds to cover any outstanding course fees for one academic year, as outlined in their Confirmation of Acceptance for Studies.

The Home Office links the maintenance requirement to the living-cost support available to domestic students through maintenance loans. This annual adjustment brings the figures in line with loan amounts for the 2026/27 academic year.

Students who have already paid for university accommodation can deduct that amount from their maintenance requirement. The maximum allowable deduction will also increase from £1,529 to £1,570.

Applicants must hold the required funds in an eligible bank account for at least 28 consecutive days. The final day of this 28-day period must fall within 31 days of the visa application date. For instance, an applicant applying on Jan. 1 must show the funds were held for 28 consecutive days ending no earlier than Dec. 1.

The requirement can also be met through qualifying student loans or official financial sponsorship.

Certain applicants are exempt from the financial requirement. This includes individuals applying as student union sabbatical officers and students who have lived in the U.K. on a valid visa for at least 12 months immediately preceding their application.

Students do not need to provide proof of funds if they are British nationals (overseas) or citizens of specific exempt countries and territories.

The exempt list includes Australia, Austria, Bahrain, Barbados, Belgium, Botswana, Brazil, Brunei, Bulgaria, Cambodia, Canada, Chile, China, Croatia, the Republic of Cyprus, Czechia, Denmark, the Dominican Republic, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hong Kong, Hungary, Iceland, Indonesia, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Kazakhstan, Kuwait, Latvia, Liechtenstein, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Macao, Malaysia, Malta, Mauritius, Mexico, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Oman, Peru, Poland, Portugal, Qatar, Romania, Serbia, Singapore, Slovakia, Slovenia, South Korea, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand, Tunisia, the United Arab Emirates, and the United States.

During the 2024/25 academic year, 685,565 international students were enrolled at U.K. higher education institutions, making up 24% of the total student population. Of these, 63,600 came from the EU and 622,000 from outside the bloc.

This total marks a 10% drop from the record high in 2022/23, representing the second consecutive year of decline. Since the current higher education sector was established in the early 1990s, international student numbers have fallen in only one other year, which was 2012/13.

Restaurant owner Loan says Tay Ho was established in 1961.

Customers can now choose rice rolls filled with shrimp, pork and mushrooms; chicken and mushrooms; shredded pork floss; or shrimp floss.

The restaurant also offers vegetarian varieties, including mixed rice rolls filled with mushrooms, lotus seeds and beans; pandan-flavored rice rolls with vegetables and tofu; and rolls filled with lingzhi mushrooms.

His childhood was split in two – before and after Roy’s death. He tells me about early memories of accompanying his father to child-friendly TV shows on which Roy was guesting. “I went to the recording of Roland Rat with Dad. Enjoyed that. The Sooty Show – enjoyed that, too. Give us a Clue – lovely.” He could see how much his father was adored, and it made him so proud.

Natalia Osipova, Rory Kinnear (second from right) and other cast members in The Standard of Living by James Graham at Theatre Royal Haymarket in London. Photograph: credit Manuel Harlan

After his father died, he got on with life – winning a scholarship to the independent school St Paul’s, excelling in music and acting, and sailing through exams thanks to his photographic memory. He says he felt he was sustained by Roy, who had lost his own father at a young age in the workplace. Kinnear’s paternal grandfather, Roy Sr, was a renowned Scottish rugby player, who represented Britain at both union and league. In 1942, at the age of 38, Roy Sr died during a match while playing for the RAF.

Did Roy, who was eight when Roy Sr died, ever talk about his father? “I remember him talking about him once, and he welled up.” A few years ago, Kinnear discovered how different it had been for his father after Roy Sr died, when his aunt told him that they were ordered not to discuss it. “My dad and his sister, my aunt, didn’t see my granny for two weeks because she was organising the funeral and then they were told you’re going to meet your mum at the station and then go back up to Scotland from Wigan to start a new life. And they were told: ‘Don’t mention your dad, don’t upset your mum.”

View image in fullscreen

Rory Kinnear in 1983 with his mother Carmel, his father Roy and his sister Kirsty. Photograph: David O’Neil/ANL/Shutterstock

Kinnear starts shaking with tears. He looks embarrassed and smiles. “It still makes me cry. And they’re just sitting on the train with my granny, and my dad’s eight and my aunt’s 10, and they’re looking out of the window trying not to mention anything. Then after about half an hour my granny goes: ‘So how have you all been since …’ and stops. And they lean forward: ‘Since what, Mum? Since what?’ And she finally says: ‘Since your father died.’” And that was that. His grandad’s death was done and dusted.

Kinnear’s grief at his own father’s death didn’t hit him for years. “By the time I was 16, I was beginning to be sad.” He was also angry because his father had died in such a stupid, unnecessary way. What went wrong? “He shouldn’t have been on the horse. The stuntmen refused to do it. It was a cobbled bridge in Spain. Someone said ‘You can’t put horses on cobbled bridges, what are you doing?’ They looked as if they were going to lose the day, so it was agreed that they’d just put some sand down and get the actors to do it.”

After his death, Kinnear’s mother discovered a letter that Roy hadn’t had time to send. “He’d written to my mum: ‘Just off to do a scene that the stuntmen said was too dangerous to do, so wish me good luck.’ It was also a hospital fuck-up. He broke his pelvis. He shouldn’t have died. He just needed a blood transfusion.” The family sued producer Pierre Spengler, director Richard Lester and the production company Falcon Films for negligence, and were awarded £650,000 in damages plus costs.

I ask Kinnear if knowing his father had gone through the same thing helped with his grief. He nods. “I went hand in hand into my grief with his grief. I felt supported and still do.” He wipes away a tear. “I’m a blubber,” he says apologetically. It also helped that he knew Roy had emerged from his grieving intact. “What I was going through he had done, and he’d become a lovely man.”

He thinks the tragedies changed them both in the same way. “There was a sense that he was unmoved by the little stuff because he had been given the big picture early. And I was given the big picture early, too. And that is in some ways a great gift.” What counts as the little stuff? “We didn’t sweat about the minutiae, the tedious irritabilities of life.”

It’s difficult to blub when you’re on the trumpet. A brass blubber, that would be a great party trick

Classical music proved a great solace, he says. When he discovered Mozart’s Dies Irae from his Requiem, he also discovered how to mourn. “I couldn’t believe how much it spoke to me.” He had played trumpet since he was four, but now he learned the piano to help to release his emotions. Did the trumpet not do the trick? He laughs. “It’s difficult to blub when you’re on the trumpet. A brass blubber, that would be a great party trick. On the tuba!” I can sense him choreographing the scene in his head as we talk.

He went on to study English literature at the University of Oxford. Did he enjoy it? “I didn’t love it, if I’m honest.” It’s not as if he wasn’t used to privilege. He’d come straight from St Paul’s, after all. But this was different, he says. “Everybody seemed to know what the plan was. I felt everyone had inherited the plan.” What was it? “Be in politics or law or business. And there was also a readiness to accept the historical tropes of the place, whether it be the rivalry between colleges or what you should do on this particular day because it’s been done for 500 years!”

View image in fullscreen

With Luke Cinque White in The Standard of Living. Photograph: Manuel Harlan

From Oxford, he went to the drama school Lamda (the London Academy of Music and Dramatic Art), where his mother had studied, and he couldn’t have been happier. “I realised I love working with other people, I loved having my day structured by someone else, so I instantly knew this is where I should be. You work off other people’s energies. That is what acting is. You are just searching within yourself doing a degree like English, whereas acting feels like a communal pursuit.”

Like Roy, he went from drama school to the Royal Shakespeare Company (RSC), and before long TV opportunities came along. By his early 30s, he had played Hamlet at the National Theatre, hogged the headlines as pig-fucking prime minister Michael Callow in Charlie Brooker’s Black Mirror, and written The Herd, a play about the 21st birthday party of a man with profounddisabilities. In 2017, he added another feather to his bow, directing the world premiere of Ryan Wigglesworth’s opera A Winter’s Tale.

View image in fullscreen

As Nicol Trowbridge in The Diplomat. Photograph: Idris Solomon/Netflix

He says he’s not quite as work obsessed as he was in his early years, but you wouldn’t know it. In 2023, he joined forces with his real-life partner, the actor Pandora Colin in the Netflix drama The Diplomat: she played his wife, Lydia. He and Colin have been together for 20 years, recently had a civil partnership, and have two children, aged 15 and 12. Colin is the daughter of real-life diplomat David Ormsby-Gore who proposed to Jackie Kennedy (before marrying Colin’s mother) and died from injuries sustained in a car crash when she was only 12.

Kinnear is perhaps best known as Bill Tanner from the Bond films. I know very little about James Bond, I say. Me too, he replies with a grin. “I hadn’t seen Bond films before doing them. It wasn’t something I watched. But I’ve enjoyed being in them, and it’s a nice night out to see the premiere! It’s good fun.” He has been in four Bond films, three of them as chief of staff to Judi Dench’s M.

View image in fullscreen

Spooked … Kinnear, Ben Whishaw and Daniel Craig in Spectre. Photograph: Courtesy/Rex Shutterstock

Is it true that Dench is his godmother? Not quite, he says. “Her husband, Michael [Williams], was my godfather. He and my dad became great mates when they were at the RSC together. I didn’t know him that well before, but when Dad died he really upped his game. Then when I was working on the Bond films with Judi, it was great not to be cowed by the legend that is, and also to be able to have those shared memories.”

He looks at a rotating deluxe Scrabble board in front of us. “She was a big Scrabble player. We played quite a bit.” A couple of days before Kinnear and I meet Dench announced that her sight was now too poor for her to work. Such sad news, he says. “Talk about people who love to act! Also, her extraordinary recall. We used to occasionally play a game where I’d give her a line from Shakespeare and see if she knew the next one. Even from roles she hadn’t played. She just knows the work inside out. I can’t remember a role two months after I’ve finished. It just goes.”

Karina wanted to go on the fastest fairground rides that we were frightened to go on. She would just be laughing her head off as we were screaming

Kinnear loves words. It was obvious when he wrote about Karina for the Guardian after she died from Covid in 2020 aged 48. One piece was a beautiful tribute in which he said her kindness remained his anchor. Anotherwas also beautiful but seared with rage after he discovered that the day he, his mother and younger sister gathered for Karina’s socially distanced funeral, Boris Johnson was indulging in a lockdown party.

What was Karina like? “Oh!” His eyes light up. “She was someone who laughed a lot. And each laugh had a slightly different meaning. As a non-verbal adult, that was her means of communication. She loved noisy things, she loved whacking things. Although her arms were quite stiff she loved to whack down on a drum, and a tambourine. She loved being pushed particularly fast in her wheelchair, and when she was younger she wanted to go on the fastest fairground rides that we were frightened to go on. She would just be laughing her head off as we were screaming.” He smiles through the memories. Throughout the day, he says, the house would be filled with Karina’s music (nursery rhymes and Abba) and when her nurses left after their shift, they would be whistling her songs.

View image in fullscreen

Master of understatement … Rory Kinnear. Photograph: Linda Nylind/The Guardian

He talks about the heroism of his mother, who not only brought up Karina but also bought a house and turned it into a home for others with profound disabilities. He says Karina politicised him. “I would see that my mum’s life as a principal caregiver was at the mercy of the decisions being made elsewhere.” Does he have confidence in Andy Burnham’s proposed social care service? “Yes, at least it’s being talked about. He’s only doing it because people are crying out for it.”

So much change is needed, he says. Not least in his own industry. He assumed that safety on film sets would have been revolutionised since his father’s death, but he discovered it hasn’t been. He is a patron of the Mark Milsome Foundation, named after the award-winning cameraman killed in 2017 when a car stunt went wrong on the Netflix and BBC drama Black Earth Rising. “The industry’s predicated on the hope of the worst not happening rather than ensuring that the worst is eliminated.” Today, cast and crew are still under pressure to cut corners, he says.

Has that happened to him? “Yay!” he says as if it’s a ridiculous question. And has he resisted the pressure? Yes, he says, but he’s in a stronger position than most. “If someone had the same qualms about safety that I could see, they might not have had the courage to say it or the lifetime experience to know that you can say no.” Has he been asked to compromise his safety more than once? “It’s probably happened four or five times. In one of my first jobs, a biblical epic, we were filming in the desert and they said today we’re going to have you riding at the top of this hill, and I was like: ‘I’m going to need riding lessons.’ They said: ‘The horse will just be walking,’ and I was like: ‘What if the horse starts going faster? I don’t feel comfortable with that.’ And it was like: ‘Mate, it’s your first job, fucking wind your neck in.’”

When he told the producers his father had died riding a horse on location they finally backed down. “I guess I have the cards to play to say no shot is worth anything as important as human life. None of it is worth what happened to me.” So you say what happened to you, not your father? “Yes because I’m standing in front of them and I’ve lived 40 years of this accident affecting my life. I’ve made it work in the best way for me, but both Dad and I would have preferred it not have happened.” Kinnear is a master of understatement.

He found it devastating in the pandemic how disabled people such as Karina were written off; their lives, and deaths, dismissed as insignificant. That’s why he was so keen to write about her. “A lot of her life was missing out on stuff or having things prodded or poked, but she was joyful and remained joyful.” Karina taught him so much, he says. “The most cherished relationships we have are the ones that most surprised us or changed us in a way we didn’t expect. Karina cherished joy despite getting the shitty end of the stick, which is a good lesson for life in itself, but she also made people around her so much happier.”

Kinnear thinks of Roy Sr who he never got to meet, Roy who was only there for his first 10 years and Karina who he knew best. They might have died young, but all three enriched, and continue to enrich, the lives of those around them. It goes back to the issue he’s spent a lifetime pondering. And he asks himself the question one last time. “It’s that thing isn’t it, what is a good life?”
